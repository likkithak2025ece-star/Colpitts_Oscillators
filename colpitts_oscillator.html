1. Introduction and learning objectives

A Colpitts oscillator is an LC sinusoidal oscillator in which the feedback network uses two capacitors in series and an inductor. The two capacitors form a capacitive voltage divider that feeds a fraction of the output signal back to the active device with the phase required to sustain oscillations.

Learning objectives

  • Explain positive feedback and sustained oscillation.
  • Identify the transistor amplifier and LC tank.
  • Calculate resonant frequency using equivalent capacitance.
  • Interpret the Multisim waveform and troubleshoot a circuit.

Prerequisites

  • Transistor biasing and small-signal gain
  • Capacitive reactance and inductive reactance
  • Resonance, phase, and feedback
  • Reading voltage waveforms on an oscilloscope

Key terminology

Tank circuit: energy exchange between L and C.
Resonance: inductive and capacitive reactances cancel.
Positive feedback: returned signal reinforces the oscillation.
Startup: noise/transients grow until limited by circuit nonlinearity.

3. Working principle

  1. DC bias is established. R1 and R2 set the base bias; R4 provides emitter-current stabilization. The transistor must operate in an active region for useful amplification.
  2. A small disturbance starts the signal. Switching on the supply creates noise and transient changes. A small component at the tank's resonant frequency is selected and reinforced.
  3. The LC network selects a frequency. Energy alternates between the magnetic field of L1 and the electric fields of the capacitors. At resonance, the tank response is strongest.
  4. Capacitive feedback sustains oscillation. C4 and C5 form a capacitive divider. A portion of the tank signal is returned with the correct phase so the amplifier replenishes energy lost in the circuit.
  5. Amplitude settles. Initially, oscillations grow. Transistor nonlinearity and circuit losses limit the amplitude; excessive limiting causes distortion.

DC versus AC behaviour

DC equivalent

Capacitors are treated as open circuits after steady state; the inductor is ideally a short. Bias resistors establish the operating point. Real capacitor leakage and inductor winding resistance are usually small but nonzero.

AC equivalent

Coupling and bypass capacitors can have low reactance at the oscillation frequency. The transistor acts as an amplifier and the LC network provides frequency selection and feedback. Parasitic capacitances and loading shift the actual frequency.

Barkhausen criterion

|Aβ| = 1 and total loop phase = 0° (or 360° × integer) at the oscillation frequency

A is amplifier gain and β is the feedback factor. For reliable startup, the small-signal loop-gain magnitude is usually slightly greater than one; nonlinear effects reduce it to approximately one after steady-state amplitude is reached.

4. Equations and derivation

Equivalent capacitance

For the two series capacitors in the ideal Colpitts tank, the equivalent capacitance is:

Ceq = (C1 C2) / (C1 + C2)

For two equal capacitors C, Ceq = C/2. Series capacitance is smaller than either individual capacitor.

Resonant frequency

f0 ≈ 1 / (2π √(L Ceq))

Here f0 is in hertz, L in henries, and Ceq in farads. This is the ideal tank estimate; transistor junction capacitances, wiring, loading, and component tolerances shift the measured frequency.

Capacitive feedback ratio

For a simple series capacitive divider, the voltage division depends on which capacitor voltage is taken as the feedback output. Do not memorize a single ratio without checking the node definitions. In a common convention, the divider ratio magnitude is approximately Csmall/(C1 + C2) for the voltage across the other capacitor; label the actual nodes before calculating β.

Reactance and period

XL = 2πfL     XC = 1/(2πfC)     T = 1/f

Engineering assumptions

  • Ideal resonance formula assumes a lossless L and C and negligible loading.
  • The transistor provides enough gain to compensate tank losses.
  • Oscillation frequency is set mainly by L and the effective series capacitance, not by the DC supply voltage.
  • The formula gives a first estimate, not a guaranteed simulation measurement.

Suggested Multisim procedure

  1. Build the circuit and verify transistor pinout and ground connections.
  2. Check the DC operating point: base, emitter, and collector voltages should be plausible for an NPN common-emitter stage.
  3. Run transient analysis long enough to include startup and steady state.
  4. Place the oscilloscope probe at the intended output node and confirm its ground reference.
  5. Measure period between adjacent peaks; calculate frequency as the reciprocal.
  6. Vary L or either tank capacitor slightly and observe the frequency shift.

6. Solved numerical examples

Example 1 — equal capacitors

Given: L = 220 µH, C1 = C2 = 1 nF. Find the ideal tank frequency, assuming these are the two tank capacitors.

Ceq = (1 nF × 1 nF)/(1 nF + 1 nF) = 0.5 nF
f0 = 1/[2π√(220 × 10−6 × 0.5 × 10−9)] ≈ 480 kHz

Answer: approximately 480 kHz. This is an ideal estimate; parasitic and loading capacitances may lower the actual frequency.

Example 2 — unequal capacitors

Given: L = 100 µH, C1 = 2.2 nF, C2 = 4.7 nF. Find Ceq and f0.

Ceq = (2.2 × 4.7)/(2.2 + 4.7) nF ≈ 1.498 nF
f0 ≈ 1/[2π√(100 × 10−6 × 1.498 × 10−9)] ≈ 411 kHz

Answer: Ceq ≈ 1.50 nF and f0 ≈ 411 kHz.

Example 3 — GATE-style frequency measurement

Given: adjacent peaks on the steady-state waveform are separated by T = 2.5 µs. Find frequency.

f = 1/T = 1/(2.5 × 10−6) = 4 × 105 Hz = 400 kHz

Answer: 400 kHz. Use the time difference between successive peaks, not the entire screen width unless the number of cycles is counted correctly.

7. GATE-style practice bank

These are original practice questions, not claimed previous-year GATE questions. Expand each question to reveal the answer and explanation.

1. [Easy] Which elements form the frequency-selective tank in a basic Colpitts oscillator?

Answer: An inductor and two capacitors

The inductor resonates with the equivalent series capacitance of the capacitive divider.

2. [Easy] The two feedback capacitors in a Colpitts oscillator mainly form a:

Answer: Capacitive voltage divider

The divider provides a fraction of the tank voltage for feedback.

3. [Easy] For two equal capacitors C connected in series, the equivalent capacitance is:

Answer: C/2

For series capacitors, 1/Ceq = 1/C + 1/C, hence Ceq = C/2.

4. [Moderate] If L = 100 µH and Ceq = 1 nF, the ideal resonant frequency is closest to:

Answer: 503 kHz

f = 1/(2π√LC) = 1/(2π√(10⁻⁴ × 10⁻⁹)) ≈ 503 kHz.

5. [Moderate] If the effective tank capacitance increases while L stays fixed, the oscillation frequency generally:

Answer: Decreases

f is inversely proportional to the square root of C.

6. [Moderate] Which criterion describes sustained sinusoidal oscillation?

Answer: Loop-gain magnitude unity and total loop phase 0°/360°

This is the Barkhausen criterion for the steady-state oscillation condition.

7. [Moderate] A measured waveform has period 5 µs. Its frequency is:

Answer: 200 kHz

f = 1/T = 1/(5 × 10⁻⁶) = 200 kHz.

8. [Moderate] Why can measured frequency differ from the ideal LC calculation?

Answer: Parasitic capacitance, loading, and component tolerances

The ideal equation ignores transistor junction capacitance, wiring, and nonideal components.

9. [Difficult] For C1 = 3 nF and C2 = 6 nF, the series equivalent capacitance is:

Answer: 2 nF

Ceq = (3 × 6)/(3 + 6) nF = 2 nF.

10. [Difficult] At startup, why is loop gain often designed slightly greater than one?

Answer: To overcome losses and grow a small initial disturbance

After amplitude grows, nonlinearities reduce effective gain until a stable amplitude is reached.

8. Core electronics interview preparation

1. What is a Colpitts oscillator?

An LC oscillator using a capacitive voltage divider for feedback. Its resonant frequency is mainly determined by the inductor and the equivalent series capacitance.

2. How does it differ from a Hartley oscillator?

Colpitts uses a capacitive divider; Hartley uses an inductive divider. Both use LC resonance.

3. State the ideal oscillation-frequency formula.

f₀ ≈ 1/(2π√(LCeq)), where Ceq = C1C2/(C1+C2) for the ideal series capacitors.

4. What is the Barkhausen criterion?

At the oscillation frequency, the loop-gain magnitude is unity and the total loop phase is an integer multiple of 360°.

5. What starts oscillation when there is no input signal?

Noise and power-on transients contain a small component near resonance that can be amplified by the positive-feedback loop.

6. Why does the amplitude not grow forever?

Transistor nonlinearities, bias shifts, and losses limit the amplitude; too much limiting distorts the waveform.

7. How would you raise the oscillation frequency?

Reduce L or reduce the effective tank capacitance, while accounting for parasitics and maintaining sufficient loop gain.

8. Why include an emitter bypass capacitor?

It reduces AC emitter degeneration over the frequency range where its reactance is sufficiently low, increasing AC gain while the emitter resistor still stabilizes DC bias.

9. The waveform is flat-topped. What would you investigate?

Check transistor bias and clipping, drive/feedback level, supply headroom, load, and oscilloscope scaling. Strong limiting can create harmonics.

10. The circuit does not oscillate in Multisim. What should you check?

Verify transistor pinout, DC operating point, component values, capacitor connections, ground, coupling paths, tank loss/loading, and transient simulation duration. Real circuits may need startup noise or a small initial condition.

11. Why does a probe change the frequency?

Probe capacitance and input resistance load the tank and change its effective capacitance and damping.

12. Where is this circuit used?

RF signal sources and local oscillators, where a simple tunable LC oscillator is suitable. High-stability systems may instead use a crystal or a PLL-referenced source.

10. Quick revision sheet

Must-remember formulas

Ceq = C1C2/(C1+C2)

f0 ≈ 1/(2π√(LCeq))

f = 1/T

XL = 2πfL; XC = 1/(2πfC)

Advantages

  • Simple LC resonant network
  • Convenient frequency tuning by changing L or C
  • Useful at radio frequencies

Limitations

  • Frequency affected by parasitics and loading
  • Stability usually lower than a crystal reference
  • Transistor nonlinearity can distort the waveform

Common errors and troubleshooting

SymptomLikely checks
No oscillationBias point, transistor pinout, feedback polarity, open/shorted tank, simulation time, and startup conditions.
Wrong frequencyRecalculate Ceq, confirm units (µH/nF), check parasitic capacitance and probe loading.
Distorted waveformCheck clipping, transistor bias, supply headroom, feedback strength, and load.
Amplitude too lowCheck transistor gain, tank losses, coupling capacitors, and excessive loading.

Industry relevance

Colpitts-style LC oscillators are useful in RF signal generation and local-oscillator stages. Engineers validate startup, frequency drift, phase noise, output amplitude, and sensitivity to load and temperature. In production equipment, oscillators may be followed by buffers, PLLs, or frequency synthesizers when stability and isolation are important.

Memory aid: Colpitts → Capacitors split the feedback. Hartley → inductors split the feedback. Always use the equivalent capacitance in the frequency formula.
